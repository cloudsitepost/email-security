How SPF Records Help Prevent
Business Email Compromise Attacks


Business Email Compromise (BEC) is a common email-based cyber threat. Attackers impersonate executives, employees, vendors, or other trusted business partners to persuade recipients to share sensitive information, transfer money, or disclose login credentials. Because these attacks can exploit gaps in email authentication, fraudulent messages may be difficult to distinguish from legitimate ones.

Sender Policy Framework (SPF) helps reduce email spoofing by identifying the mail servers authorized to send messages on behalf of a domain. Receiving servers can use this information to identify unauthorized senders. SPF cannot stop every BEC attack, but, when combined with DKIM and DMARC, it provides an important layer of protection.

Here are seven ways SPF records can help businesses reduce the risk of Business Email Compromise attacks.


1. Prevents Unauthorized Email Spoofing


Email spoofing is a common tactic in business email compromise (BEC) attacks. Cybercriminals forge a sender address to make a message look like it came from a legitimate company domain. For example, an attacker might pose as a finance manager and ask an employee to make an urgent payment.

SPF can help reduce this risk by specifying which servers are authorized to send email for a domain. Administrators publish an SPF record as a TXT record in the domain’s DNS settings. It lists the IP addresses and third-party email services permitted to send messages on the domain’s behalf.

When a mail server receives a message, it can compare the sending server’s IP address with the domain’s SPF policy. If the server isn’t authorized, the message may fail SPF authentication. 





2. Identifies Unauthorized Email Senders


BEC attackers often send fraudulent emails from unauthorized infrastructure that appears to use trusted business addresses. Without proper email authentication, receiving servers may struggle to identify suspicious senders.

An SPF record helps administrators define which systems can legitimately send emails using their domain. These systems may include:

  • Company-hosted email servers.

  • Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace.

  • Marketing automation platforms.

  • Customer relationship management (CRM) systems.

  • Transactional email service providers.

Keeping your SPF record accurate helps receiving servers identify messages sent from unauthorized IP addresses. Review authorized senders regularly and remove outdated entries or services that no longer send email on your organization’s behalf to maintain a more controlled email environment.


3. Reduces Domain Impersonation Risks


Domain impersonation happens when attackers send messages from addresses that resemble a legitimate business domain. These fraudulent requests may target employees, customers, vendors, or finance teams. SPF helps protect an organization’s domain by specifying which sources are authorized to send email. If an attacker sends a message from an unauthorized server using the organization’s domain in the envelope sender address, SPF can detect the mismatch.

However, SPF does not check every sender address a recipient sees. It primarily authenticates the domain in the SMTP MAIL FROM identity, which may differ from the address in the email client’s visible From field. For stronger protection against direct domain spoofing, organizations should use DMARC with SPF alignment requirements.





4. Improves Email Deliverability and Filtering


Business email compromise (BEC) attacks often begin with fraudulent messages reaching employees’ inboxes. Email authentication gives receiving systems a way to assess whether incoming messages come from authorized sources. When a legitimate business email passes SPF authentication, receiving servers get an additional signal that its sending infrastructure is authorized. This can support email filtering and domain reputation management.

To avoid authentication errors, organizations should include every legitimate email service in their SPF records. If an authorized provider is missing, genuine messages may fail SPF checks. For example, a company that uses Microsoft 365 for internal email and a separate provider for marketing campaigns should configure its SPF policy to account for both.


5. Supports DMARC Protection Against BEC Attacks


SPF provides useful authentication information, but it doesn’t tell receiving servers to reject every message that isn’t authorized. DMARC addresses this by working with SPF and DKIM to check authentication results and domain alignment. Domain owners can publish a DMARC policy that tells receiving servers how to handle messages that fail DMARC authentication.

The three primary DMARC policies are:

  • p=none: Monitors authentication results without requesting enforcement.

  • p=quarantine: Requests that suspicious messages be placed in spam or quarantine.

  • p=reject: Requests that messages failing DMARC checks be rejected.

Organizations can start by monitoring email activity, then strengthen enforcement once they’ve identified their legitimate sending sources. Using SPF with DMARC helps prevent unauthorized parties from successfully impersonating the organization’s domain, reducing opportunities for some business email compromise (BEC) attacks.
