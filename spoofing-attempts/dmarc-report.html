How To Identify Email Spoofing Attempts
Through DMARC Reports


Email spoofing occurs when attackers impersonate a legitimate domain to send fraudulent messages. Because these emails may appear to come from trusted businesses, employees, or service providers, they can be difficult to recognize. DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance) helps domain owners monitor SPF and DKIM authentication results. By reviewing DMARC reports, administrators can identify unauthorized senders, investigate suspicious activity, and strengthen domain security.


1. Understand How DMARC Reports Work


DMARC reports show how email receivers assess messages claiming to come from your domain. They include details about SPF and DKIM authentication, domain alignment, sending IP addresses, and DMARC policy results.

There are two primary types of DMARC reports:

  • Aggregate Reports (RUA): Provide summarized information about email traffic, including sending IP addresses, message volumes, SPF and DKIM results, and policy evaluations.

  • Forensic Reports (RUF): Provide detailed information about individual messages that fail DMARC authentication, where supported by receiving providers.

Aggregate reports are particularly useful for identifying suspicious sending sources and unusual email activity across a domain.


2. Monitor Unknown Sending IP Addresses


Check the sending IP addresses in DMARC aggregate reports to help identify email spoofing attempts. The reports show which IPs send messages using your domain in the visible From address. Compare those IPs with your approved email sources, such as corporate mail servers, marketing platforms, CRM systems, and third-party email providers.





Look for the following warning signs:

  • Unknown IP addresses sending emails on behalf of your domain.

  • Unexpected sending activity from unfamiliar geographic locations.

  • Large volumes of messages originating from unauthorized servers.

  • Repeated DMARC failures associated with the same IP address.

If an unfamiliar IP address appears in multiple reports, investigate its source before determining whether it represents malicious activity or an overlooked legitimate sender.


3. Analyze SPF and DKIM Authentication Failures


SPF and DKIM are key parts of DMARC authentication. Reviewing their results helps administrators tell legitimate email delivery issues apart from potential spoofing attempts.

  • SPF failures: SPF checks whether the sending IP address is authorized to send email for the domain in the envelope sender. If it isn’t, this may indicate a spoofing attempt.

  • DKIM failures: DKIM checks the cryptographic signature attached to an email. If verification fails, the email may have been sent without authorization, DKIM may be misconfigured, or the message may have been altered during delivery.

Check whether SPF and DKIM align with the visible From domain. Either can pass on its own while DMARC fails if the authenticated domain doesn’t align with the From address. Investigate repeated authentication failures from unknown sources.


4. Identify Suspicious Email Volume Patterns


Unexpected spikes in email volume can signal a spoofing campaign. DMARC aggregate reports let administrators track message counts by sending IP address. If a domain that typically sends a few hundred emails a day suddenly shows thousands of messages from unfamiliar servers, it may indicate an impersonation attempt.

Pay attention to these patterns:

  • Sudden increases in messages from unauthorized IP addresses.

  • Repeated authentication failures within a short period.

  • Multiple unknown sources using the same organizational domain.

  • Unusual sending activity outside normal business operations.

Monitoring these patterns regularly helps security teams identify suspicious activity before attackers can exploit domain reputation.





5. Check DMARC Policy Evaluation Results


DMARC reports show which policy was applied to messages that fail authentication. Administrators should verify that receiving servers are enforcing the configured policy correctly.

The three primary DMARC policies are:

  • p=none: Monitors email authentication without requesting message rejection or quarantine.

  • p=quarantine: Requests that receiving servers treat failing messages as suspicious, commonly placing them in spam.

  • p=reject: Requests that receiving servers reject messages failing DMARC evaluation.

A domain set to p=none can use reports to identify spoofing attempts, but it does not ask receiving systems to enforce action against unauthorized messages. Administrators should review authentication results, investigate legitimate sending sources, and gradually move to quarantine or reject once their email infrastructure is properly configured.


6. Use DMARC Monitoring Tools for Investigation


Manually reviewing XML-based DMARC reports can take time, particularly when you manage multiple domains or receive reports from many email providers. DMARC monitoring tools make the data easier to review by turning raw XML into readable dashboards and visual summaries. Visit www.duocircle.com. for more details.

Some commonly used tools include:

  • DMARCReport: Helps analyze aggregate reports, identify unauthorized senders, and monitor authentication performance.

  • dmarcian: Provides DMARC reporting and domain authentication visibility.

  • EasyDMARC: Offers reporting dashboards and authentication monitoring.

  • MXToolbox: Provides DNS and email authentication diagnostic tools.
