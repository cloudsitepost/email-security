Can Sender Policy Framework Protect Businesses
From Business Email Compromise?

Business Email Compromise (BEC) is a serious email-based threat. Attackers may pose as executives, employees, vendors, or other trusted partners to persuade recipients to transfer money, share confidential information, or disclose login credentials. Sender Policy Framework (SPF) helps organizations prevent unauthorized senders from spoofing their domains, but it cannot stop every BEC attack. Knowing what SPF can and cannot do helps businesses build stronger email security strategies.


What Is Sender Policy Framework and How Does It Work?


Sender Policy Framework (SPF) is an email authentication protocol that lets domain owners specify which mail servers may send email on their behalf. They publish these approved sources—such as IP addresses and third-party email services—in a DNS TXT record.

When a receiving mail server gets a message, it checks the domain in the sender’s Return-Path against that domain’s SPF record, then compares the sending server’s IP address with the sources the record authorizes.

For example:

`v=spf1 ip4:203.0.113.5 include:_spf.someprovider.com -all`

This record authorizes the listed IP address and the email service referenced by the `include` mechanism. The `-all` mechanism indicates that sources not otherwise authorized should fail SPF authentication. Depending on the record and the receiving server’s evaluation, the result may be PASS, FAIL, SOFTFAIL, NEUTRAL, or NONE.

The Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) standardized SPF; RFC 7208 is its current core specification. SPF helps organizations reduce unauthorized use of their domains and strengthen email authentication.





How Business Email Compromise Works


Business email compromise (BEC) is a targeted cyberattack in which criminals impersonate or manipulate business communications to steal money or sensitive information. Unlike ordinary spam, BEC attacks typically rely on careful research and convincing impersonation.

Attackers commonly use the following methods:

  • Executive impersonation: Criminals pretend to be company executives and request urgent financial transactions.

  • Vendor fraud: Attackers impersonate suppliers and send fraudulent invoices with modified banking information.

  • Email account compromise: Cybercriminals gain access to legitimate employee accounts and send deceptive messages.

  • Lookalike domains: Attackers register domains that closely resemble genuine business domains.

  • Social engineering: Fraudsters create urgency or pressure employees into sharing confidential information.

These attacks can appear legitimate because criminals often imitate familiar communication styles and business processes.


How SPF Helps Protect Businesses From BEC


SPF provides an important layer of protection by helping receiving mail servers identify unauthorized sending sources.


1. Preventing Direct Domain Spoofing

Attackers may spoof a company’s domain in the Return-Path address to make fraudulent messages look legitimate. A correctly configured SPF record lets receiving servers check whether the sending IP address is authorized. If it isn’t, the message may fail SPF authentication and, depending on the recipient’s security policies, be rejected, quarantined, or flagged as suspicious.


2. Improving Email Trust and Deliverability

SPF helps receiving mail servers verify that messages come from authorized senders. This supports your domain’s reputation and lowers the risk of legitimate business emails being marked as spam. If your organization uses multiple email service providers, make sure each one is authorized in your SPF record.

Why SPF Alone Cannot Stop Business Email Compromise


Although SPF provides valuable protection, it has several limitations that businesses must understand.





1. It Does Not Authenticate the Visible From Address

SPF checks the envelope sender domain—not the From address people see in their email client. Attackers can exploit this difference to make messages appear to come from company executives. DMARC helps by checking whether SPF or DKIM authentication aligns with the visible From domain.


2. Lookalike Domains Can Pass SPF

Cybercriminals can register domains that closely resemble legitimate business addresses, then publish valid SPF records and send authenticated emails from them. Because SPF checks whether a sender is authorized—not whether the domain is trustworthy—it cannot detect this kind of impersonation on its own.


3. Compromised Accounts Can Bypass SPF Protection

If attackers access a legitimate employee or executive email account, they can send messages through an authorized mail server, allowing SPF to pass. Additional account security measures are needed to detect suspicious activity.


4. Email Forwarding Can Cause Authentication Failures

Email forwarding can cause SPF authentication problems when the forwarding server’s IP address isn’t listed in the original sender’s SPF record. Sender Rewriting Scheme (SRS) can help by changing the envelope sender during forwarding.
